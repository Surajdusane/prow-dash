## The modeling question

The schema is shaped around the decisions the dashboard has to support:

- **Area-level demand** — where field capacity is under strain, and which areas
  need more technicians.
- **Household-level churn** — which households are at risk, and what evidence
  supports that score.
- **Device-level telemetry** — which dispensers are degrading, offline, or due
  for a filter change.
- **Subscription-level revenue** — what MRR is, how plan mix is shifting, and
  which payments are late.
- **Society-level expansion** — which apartment complexes are penetrated, and
  where the next growth wave should go.

Every table earns its place by supporting one of those decisions. The domain
chain is the spine: a society sits in an area, households sit in societies,
subscriptions attach households to plans, dispensers attach to households, and
readings attach to dispensers. Service, complaints, payments, and leads hang off
that spine.

| `areas`           | Geographic operating zones | `name`, `city`, `infrastructure_age_years`                                                                   |
| `societies`       | Apartment complexes        | `area_id`, `name`, `total_flats`, `onboarded_date`, `latitude`, `longitude`                                  |
| `plans`           | Product catalogue          | `name`, `monthly_price`, `deposit`, `includes_dispenser`                                                     |
| `technicians`     | Field workforce            | `area_id`, `name`, `join_date`, `active`                                                                     |
| `households`      | Customers                  | `society_id`, `flat_number`, `resident_name`, `join_date`, `status`, `cancel_date`, `risk_score`             |
| `subscriptions`   | Commercial relationship    | `household_id`, `plan_id`, `start_date`, `end_date`, `status`, `billing_cycle`                               |
| `dispensers`      | Installed device           | `household_id`, `type`, `install_date`, `last_filter_change_date`, `is_online`                               |
| `readings`        | Telemetry                  | `dispenser_id`, `recorded_at`, `tds_ppm`, `ph`, `hardness_mgl`, `purity_pct`                                 |

## The relationships

```text
areas 1─* societies 1─* households
households 1─* subscriptions *─1 plans
households 1─* dispensers 1─* readings
households 1─* service_tickets *─1 technicians
households 1─* complaints
households 1─* payments *─1 subscriptions
areas 1─* technicians
leads (standalone)
```

The chain is mostly one-to-many, so there are no join tables. A society has many
households; a household has many subscriptions, dispensers, tickets, complaints,
and payments. Plans and technicians are lookup tables on the many side.
`service_tickets` is the exception: it points at both the household and the
specific dispenser, so a repair can be tied to the physical unit that failed.

`payments` points at both the household and the subscription. The household
makes the payment; the subscription explains which plan and price it belongs to,
so a plan change or rejoin keeps its billing history without rewriting rows.

### One active subscription per household

A household buys one plan at a time. The table preserves history with
`start_date` and `end_date`: a cancellation closes the span, and a rejoin opens
a new one. The risk table reads the latest subscription per household with
`DISTINCT ON (household_id)` ordered by `start_date` descending.

There is no partial unique index enforcing “one active subscription per
household.” The invariant lives in the seed and mutation code. At one row per
household this is a safe simplification; a partial unique index on
`(household_id) WHERE status = 'active'` would enforce it if rejoins became
common.

### One dispenser per household

The service installs one unit per flat, so the seed creates exactly one
dispenser per household. The foreign key is still one-to-many: a replacement
device would add a second row rather than overwrite the old one, preserving the
old device's readings and tickets. If a household could genuinely run two
devices at once, the model would need a join table or an explicit `is_primary`
flag; today the UI assumes the single active unit.

### Readings belong to a dispenser, not a household

Telemetry is a property of the physical device. If a dispenser is replaced, the
reading history stays with the old device instead of silently attaching to the
new one. That is why `readings.dispenser_id` is the only parent key and why the
fleet queries join through `dispensers` to reach the household.

### Tickets and complaints are separate

A complaint is the customer's voice: category, severity, date, resolved flag. A
ticket is the operational response: type, technician, SLA outcome, raised and
resolved dates. One can exist without the other. A customer can complain without
a ticket being raised, and a preventive filter change can be ticketed without a
complaint. Linking them with a foreign key would lose that distinction and force
a one-to-one story that the operation does not have.

Tickets also carry `dispenser_id`; complaints do not. A complaint is about the
service experience, not necessarily a specific device.

### Leads are standalone

A lead may not have a society yet, and a converted lead can create multiple
households across a complex. `society_name` is free text, and there is no
foreign key to `societies`. A foreign key would over-constrain the funnel before
the society exists. The trade-off is that lead-to-society matching is a human
step, not a database join.

## Type decisions

- **`date` for business dates** — `join_date`, `cancel_date`, `billing_month`,
  `last_filter_change_date`, `raised_date`, `resolved_date`, `visit_date`. These
  are calendar days, compared as strings in `YYYY-MM-DD` form.
- **`timestamp` for telemetry and created-at** — `readings.recorded_at` and the
  `created_at` defaults on areas, societies, households, and leads. These need
  ordering within a day.
- **Integer rupees, not floats** — `plans.monthly_price`, `plans.deposit`, and
  `payments.amount` are integers. No rounding drift in money.
- **Enums for closed sets** — plan name, household status, subscription status,
  dispenser type, ticket type, ticket status, complaint category, complaint
  severity, payment status, and lead role. The database rejects invalid states
  before a query sees them.
- **`decimal` for measurements** — `readings.ph` is `decimal(3,1)` and
  `purity_pct` is `decimal(4,1)`. These are sensor values, not money, and the
  decimal scale is part of the reading.
- **Tri-state SLA outcome** — `service_tickets.sla_met` is nullable. `null`
  means the ticket is not resolved (or was reopened), `true` means resolved
  within target, `false` means missed. There is no `sla_hours` column:
  <SourceLink
    path="src/db/queries/tickets.ts"
    symbol="SLA_HOURS"
    label="SLA_HOURS"
  />
  defines the targets as code constants, and the resolved row stores the boolean
  outcome.
- **Denormalized `risk_score`** — the score lives on `households` as an integer
  so the risk table can sort and filter without recomputing the score for every
  row.
  <SourceLink
    path="src/db/queries/risk.ts"
    symbol="recomputeAllRiskScores"
    label="recomputeAllRiskScores"
  />
  writes the score; the seed calls it, and the `customers.recomputeRisk`
  mutation exposes it.

The denormalization is the main type trade-off. Reads are fast and the table can
sort by a real column, but the stored score goes stale when complaints,
payments, or tickets change. The app makes that explicit: resolving a complaint
does not silently re-score the household. An operator clicks **Recompute risk**,
and the same function that seeded the scores rewrites them. The alternative —
scoring on every read — would turn a paginated table into a 1,150-row scoring
job per request.

## Indexes

The indexes follow the access paths in `src/db/queries`. They were added in the
second migration, after the query shapes were known:

| Index                             | Columns                               | Why                                                     |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| `societies_area_idx`              | `societies(area_id)`                  | Area joins, penetration, and society counts per area    |
| `households_society_idx`          | `households(society_id)`              | Society → households and society detail                 |
| `households_status_idx`           | `households(status)`                  | Active/cancelled filters across KPIs and the risk table |
| `subscriptions_status_idx`        | `subscriptions(status)`               | Active subscription counts and MRR spans                |
| `subscriptions_household_idx`     | `subscriptions(household_id)`         | Latest subscription per household and history           |
| `subscriptions_plan_idx`          | `subscriptions(plan_id)`              | Plan mix joins                                          |
| `dispensers_household_idx`        | `dispensers(household_id)`            | Household → dispenser lookup                            |
| `dispensers_online_idx`           | `dispensers(is_online)`               | Fleet online/offline gauge                              |
| `readings_dispenser_recorded_idx` | `readings(dispenser_id, recorded_at)` | Latest reading and trend windows per dispenser          |
| `readings_recorded_idx`           | `readings(recorded_at)`               | Global recent-reading windows and anomaly scans         |
| `service_tickets_status_idx`      | `service_tickets(status)`             | Kanban columns and open-ticket counts                   |
| `service_tickets_raised_date_idx` | `service_tickets(raised_date)`        | 90-day SLA and area-demand windows                      |
| `service_tickets_technician_idx`  | `service_tickets(technician_id)`      | Technician workload                                     |
| `complaints_household_date_idx`   | `complaints(household_id, date)`      | Household complaint history and risk windows            |
| `complaints_date_idx`             | `complaints(date)`                    | 12-month complaint trend                                |
| `payments_billing_month_idx`      | `payments(billing_month)`             | Monthly payment status and overdue windows              |
| `payments_subscription_idx`       | `payments(subscription_id)`           | Payment ↔ subscription reconciliation                   |
| `payments_household_idx`          | `payments(household_id)`              | Household payment history                               |
| `leads_visit_date_idx`            | `leads(visit_date)`                   | Pipeline by visit date                                  |
| `leads_converted_idx`             | `leads(converted)`                    | Funnel conversion filters                               |

Some indexes are deliberately missing. There is no index on
`households.risk_score`; at roughly 1,150 households, sorting the risk table is
not the bottleneck. The first index I would add at 10x is a partial index on
active high-risk households, or a simple `risk_score` index if the filter mix
stayed broad.

There is also no `service_tickets(household_id)` index, even though the
household drawer filters tickets by household. The current indexes cover status,
raised date, and technician, not that path. With about 7,000 tickets the scan is
cheap, but it is the most obvious missing index in the schema.

The lead list orders by `created_at` descending, and there is no
`leads(created_at)` index. At 450 leads it does not matter. Both gaps are
recorded here rather than hidden behind a claim that every access path is
indexed.

## Why no snapshot tables

MRR, plan mix, and retention are reconstructed from subscription spans rather
than stored as monthly snapshots. `getMrrTrend` and `getPlanMixTrend` fetch the
spans once and fold them over the month ends; `getCohortRetention` derives
active membership from `join_date` and `cancel_date`. There is one source of
truth and no nightly job in a demo that has no scheduler.

The trade-off is CPU and transfer on every read: the aggregation happens in
TypeScript over a small book. At a much larger scale, a monthly snapshot table
would be cheaper and would preserve history after a subscription row is
corrected. Today the live reconstruction keeps the seed honest: the same
subscription rows produce MRR and plan mix.

## Trade-offs

- **One dispenser per household is a simplification.** The FK allows many; the
  seed and UI assume one. Replacements are representable, simultaneous devices
  are not.
- **No soft deletes.** `serviceOps.remove` calls `removeTicket` and deletes the
  row. That keeps the demo clean but loses the ability to audit a deleted
  ticket.
- **No audit trail.** Mutations overwrite state; there is no `updated_by`,
  `updated_at`, or event table.
- **No tenant column.** Every table belongs to the single demo dataset. Adding
  multi-tenancy would touch every table and every query.
- **No geospatial index.** `societies` stores latitude and longitude, but
  nothing queries by distance; area is the operational unit.
- **No partial unique indexes.** “One active subscription” and “one dispenser”
  are conventions, not database constraints.
- **Risk scores can lag.** The stored score changes only on recompute, so the
  risk table can show yesterday's ranking until someone clicks the button.
